Opening Gulf manufacturing through steel: the decisions at the plant's edge

Construction is the industry I know best, so when I looked for a new sector in the Gulf, I started there.

Gulf projects rarely wait on their own sites for long. They wait on what has to arrive: steel, façades, switchgear. So I followed those deliveries back to where they are made, and steel came first. Construction absorbs most of the region's steel, and a small number of mills produce it. When one of those mills changes its plan, projects feel it within weeks.

Inside the mills, I expected to find old systems. I found strong ones. Each furnace has its process models, and the meltshop, where liquid steel is made and cast, has scheduling software built by the same companies that build the plants. The gap sat somewhere else: at the plant's edge, where the gas supply, a shipment of iron ore or a customer's date changes, and someone has to decide who gets the steel that is now scarce.

The meltshop recovers within hours, because its software is built for that. The third step takes days. It needs the order book, the stock, the gas outlook, the shipping schedule and the customers' dates at once, and no single system holds them together. That decision is the subject of this proposal.

2

Why steel, and why now

Steel sits closest to the projects, and the way Gulf mills make steel ties them to inputs from outside the plant.

In Saudi Arabia, industry estimates put construction's share of steel use at about 95 per cent. Supply is concentrated: one UAE producer makes more than 60 per cent of the country's rebar. A change in one mill's plan therefore reaches many sites.

Most Gulf mills make steel from iron ore pellets and natural gas, by direct reduction, and then melt it in electric arc furnaces. That route ties the plant to three outside inputs: pellets that arrive by sea, gas from the national supplier and power from the grid. A mill that melts local scrap depends far less on gas and imported ore, so in the Gulf, more of the plant's important decisions start outside its fence.

Those decisions now carry more weight. Saudi Arabia's national industrial strategy targets about 36,000 factories by 2035, up from about 10,700 in 2022, which means more grades and more customers competing for the same output. The 2026 war showed how quickly shipping and energy can become operating constraints that force a plant to replan. And as gas, power and carbon tighten, the plan that uses them best is worth more each year.

I first saw a factory as a chain of booked capacity at a garment group in Egypt, where one failed dye lot cost the print slot behind it, then the inspection, then the shipment. A steel plant has the same shape, with much less slack.

3

The plant's systems stop at its edge

Inside a Gulf mill, every layer of the operation has software, usually from the company that built the plant. The decisions that cross the plant's edge have none.

Control systems keep each furnace stable, along with the caster that turns liquid steel into solid billets or slabs. Process models predict temperature and chemistry at each step. Above them, meltshop schedulers decide which heat, meaning one furnace load of liquid steel, goes where and when. Primetals, PSI, Danieli and ABB all sell schedulers that react to delays and account for energy. Danieli built large parts of EMSTEEL and Qatar Steel, and Ezz Steel in Egypt chose Danieli's system for both its meltshops this year. On the plant floor, a newcomer would be competing with the plant's own builder.

The edge is different. When the gas allocation drops, the scheduler can rebuild the heat plan for lower output, but it cannot decide which customer's steel ships late. That choice depends on the order book, yard stock, gas and ore availability, shipping schedules, customer dates and, in a group, capacity at the other plants. In the conversations, it came up as a negotiation between what production can do and what sales needs, worked through trackers, spreadsheets and meetings.

A steel producer's operations lead drew the same boundary. The plant was already investing in advanced scheduling, so a platform that only connected data and displayed the operation would add little. What would be of interest instead was replanning around energy, allocating scarce output to customers during a disruption, and coordinating across plants. The lead also called energy a production input rather than a background service.

Published research points the same way. A 2026 review, led by a researcher at a major Chinese steel group's technology centre, finds that AI in steel still centres on single processes, and that coordination across operations and trade-offs between competing objectives remain weak.

ERP and planning suites hold the orders and plan in weekly or monthly cycles, and global AI platforms connect enterprise data but are built and governed outside the region. An enterprise software seller in the Kingdom named the incumbent that matters most: the planner's spreadsheet beside the ERP.

One decision, and what a system must know to support it

When an input from outside the plant changes, which commitments does the producer protect, at which plant, and how does the production plan move?

Answering that needs one connected view of the plant, its commitments and the outside constraints acting on it. Today those pieces sit in different places.

The design rests on five choices. They follow the approach the same 2026 review sets out for steel: models built on how the process physically works, corrected by plant data, solving for several objectives at once, with explanations an engineer can check.

Physics sets what is possible. Hard limits come from the plant's own systems and process models: how much direct reduction can run on reduced gas, how long steel can wait between units, which grades each customer accepts. The meltshop scheduler already knows what the plant can produce under a given limit, so the system asks it rather than rebuilding it. An option that breaks a physical limit is never shown.

Plant history corrects what the plan misses. Plans assume standard times and yields, and plants rarely achieve them. Learning from the plant's history adjusts each option for how long a campaign change really takes, how yields fall when gas is short, and how each customer has responded to a moved date.

Trade-offs stay visible. Tonnes, energy, cost and commitments pull in different directions, so the system returns two or three options that balance them differently instead of one answer that hides the trade. Constraints are met first and objectives weighed second; the review notes that scheduling methods which only chase a single score need constraints built in explicitly to work in steel.

Decisions are kept with their reasons. Every option offered, the one chosen and the reason for any override are stored and linked to the orders they affected. If a planner rejects an option because a particular customer has to be protected, that reason is recorded with the decision and informs later options. Over time, judgment that now lives in a few people's heads becomes part of the plant's record.

Every option can be checked. Each one shows the limit that binds it, the data it rests on and how confident its forecast is, so an engineer can test it against the plant rather than trust a score. The system reads from the plant's systems and from outside sources, writes back only a plan a planner has approved, and never touches the control systems.

5

The buyer's side of the same decision

Switchgear shows the problem from the customer's side.

Electrical packages often sit on a project's critical path; a contractor's project-controls lead ranked them first among the packages that threaten it. A switchgear maker facing scarce components or test capacity has to decide which project to protect. Its operations lead described giving imported components to the project where they unlock the most important milestone, such as power to a building, rather than to the first order in the queue.

The buyer does not need a live view of the factory. A developer's programme lead said what matters is confidence that the commitment will hold, and listed what that means. Those items match gates a plant already records, so the same model can share the evidence behind a delivery forecast without exposing the plant.

The contractor's lead gave the measure that matters: the time between the first evidence of a problem and a changed forecast. Steel is the harder operational problem; electrical equipment makes the customer's value easier to see.

How it reaches a plant

The first engagement covers one plant and one kind of event, chosen in discovery for how often it happens and what it costs. A replay of a past event comes first, before any contract or live connection.

Replay5 days

The plant exports the records of one recent disruption, such as a gas curtailment or a delayed ore shipment: the notices, schedule versions, orders, stock and the decisions that followed. Over five days the team reconstructs the event, generates the options that existed at each decision point and compares them with what happened. The result is a scoped first engagement, with the missing constraints exposed before any live integration.

Weeks 1 to 3Discovery

Sit with planning, sales and procurement to identify the recurring events, what each costs, the data behind each decision and who owns the outcome. Audit data quality early: the 2023 review of machine learning in steelmaking notes that preparing plant data usually takes longer than building the model. Agree the owner, the sponsor and the measures of success before building.

Weeks 3 to 6Build

Build the model for one plant and one event type, using the existing scheduler for production feasibility and adding orders, stock and the outside constraints.

Weeks 6 to 12Shadow mode

For each real event, the system offers two or three feasible responses. Planners decide as usual. A weekly review goes through every case where they chose something else, and records why.

Weeks 12 to 16Live support

Planners see the options when an event arrives and approve one. Results are measured against the replay baseline: commitments met, tonnes and energy lost, and the time from the first notice to a decision.

After week 16Expansion

Add the next event type, then other plants and logistics. The object model is reused; plant-specific constraints are rebuilt. The team stays, because models drift as plant data changes.

An adviser on how large industrial companies buy operations software said pilots rarely fail because of the model. They fail because nobody owns the outcome, the data cannot be reached, a connection to plant systems was never cleared, or nobody agreed what success meant. The replay exposes most of those problems before a full deployment begins, and ownership is agreed at the start.

Who to approach, and in what order

Eight steel producers make up the first map. The first accounts are the ones I can reach credibly, not the largest.

I grade each path in by how likely it is to open a door: direct, a contact who knows the decision owner; credible, a contact who does business with the company and can introduce us; possible, a shared network; and structural, an ownership or investor link and nothing more. The line that matters is between a real introduction and a link that only looks useful on an organisation chart.

ProducerOwner and positionWhat is known about its systemsWho to reachPathFit

EMSTEEL comes first. Its rebar reaches a large share of UAE projects, and contractors who buy from it offer a credible route in. I would pair it with one privately owned Saudi mill, where the approval path should be shorter, and aim to reach both in the first three months. Hadeed and Qatar Steel are larger and more complex, but today the only links are structural, so they come next, with a reference, in months three to nine. The remaining mills, and then the aluminium smelters, follow.

In every first meeting, the opening question is which decisions the plant's existing scheduler and ERP leave to people. The answers may reorder this list.

Three working rules shape the approach. Start with the person who owns the problem; the buying adviser put it plainly, saying that who signs comes second. Begin vendor registration and the security review on day one, because each takes months. And make the first producer a design partner, with a qualified local integrator holding the contract if registration would otherwise delay it.

The first meeting

The first meeting is with the head of planning, with the chief operating officer in the room if possible. This is what I would say.

For the head of planning and the COO of an integrated steel producer

Your meltshop is in good hands. When something changes on the floor, your scheduler rebuilds the heat plan within minutes. The harder decisions start outside the fence: a cut in your gas allocation, an ore vessel that arrives late, a customer who needs steel sooner. Then planning, sales and procurement decide whose order moves, usually over several meetings and spreadsheets.

We build a model of your plant that knows what it can make under each limit, your orders and commitments, your stock and the outside inputs that just changed. When an event arrives, it offers your planners two or three feasible responses and what each costs in tonnes, energy and commitments. Your planners decide. The system records what they choose and why they reject the alternatives, so later options reflect more of how your plant actually decides. It reads from your scheduler and your ERP, and it never touches your control systems.

Give us the records of one recent disruption and five days. We will replay that week and show you the options that existed.

The ask: one recent disruption, its records, and two planners for five days.

What they will say, and the answer

“Our scheduler already handles disruptions.”
On the plant floor, it does, and it stays. This system asks the scheduler what the plant can make, then works on who receives it.
“Connecting our data and showing our operations is too generic.”
Agreed. The first engagement covers one event type at one plant, measured in commitments, tonnes and energy.
“Nothing touches our control systems.”
Correct. The system reads, and writes back only a plan that a planner approves.

What travels to the next plant

A sector is worth opening only if the second plant starts further ahead than the first. The structure travels. The numbers are rebuilt.

The 2023 review found that models trained on one plant's data transfer poorly to another, so the reusable asset is the structure of the problem rather than any trained model.

Travels

  • The object model of a plant and its edge
  • The catalogue of events: gas cuts, power limits, ore delays, route closures, changed customer dates
  • The allocation logic and the cost of each response
  • The replay kit and the discovery questions
  • Connections to the common schedulers and ERPs

Rebuilt at each plant

  • The plant's capacity under each limit, and its costs
  • Its customers, contracts and priorities
  • The unwritten rules of its planners
  • Its data connections and their fixes

The closest next segment is aluminium, where imported alumina and power constraints create a similar planning problem; EGA, Alba, Ma'aden, Qatalum and Sohar Aluminium form that map.

The GTM work carries over too: the buyer map, the objection log, the replay format and the demo structure. The target chart and table on this page already run from one dataset, with the same fields for every producer.
